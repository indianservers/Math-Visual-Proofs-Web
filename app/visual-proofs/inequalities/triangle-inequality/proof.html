When AB + BC > AC, the broken path is strictly longer than the straight path.
Visual Proof (Why the straight path is shortest)
Step 1 Drop Perpendicular
BD \perp AC
Drop perpendicular line from vertex B to base AC at point D.
Step 2 Right-Triangle Leg Bound
AB > AD \text{ and } BC > DC
In right triangles \Delta ABD and \Delta CBD, hypotenuse > leg.
Step 3 Add Inequalities
AB + BC > AD + DC
Add the two inequalities together side by side.
Step 4 (Conclusion) Theorem Established
AB + BC > AC
Since AD + DC = AC, we conclude AB + BC > AC!

Why it works 💡

1 Build the two-segment path

Travelling from point A to C through intermediate vertex B gives the broken two-segment path:

A \to B \to C \implies \text{Length} = AB + BC
For current triangle: AB + BC = 4.2 + 3.7 = 7.90
2 Compare with the straight path

Segment AC is the direct straight-line distance between A and C. Dropping a perpendicular from B to AC at point D splits the base into AD = x and DC = y:

AC = AD + DC = x + y
Base AC = 2.80 + 4.00 = 6.80
3 Apply right-triangle inequality

Triangles ΔABD and ΔCBD are right triangles. In any right triangle, the hypotenuse is strictly longer than either leg:

In ΔABD: AB > AD ⇒ a > x
In ΔCBD: BC > DC ⇒ b > y

Adding the two inequalities side-by-side:

AB + BC > AD + DC \implies \boxed{AB + BC > AC}
Live verification: 7.90 > 6.80 ⇒ Difference = +1.10
4 Degenerate vs Nondegenerate

Strict inequality AB + BC > AC holds for any nondegenerate triangle (where B does not lie on segment AC). If B lies directly on segment AC, then AB + BC = AC.
